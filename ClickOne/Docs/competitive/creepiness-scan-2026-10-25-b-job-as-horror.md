# Creepiness scan B: the job or shift as the vehicle for dread
Date: 2026-10-25

Scope: cursory summary scan of four games for "Noted: I Understand Your Frustration". Public data only (wikis, store pages, reviews); nothing played. No sales figures included. Search quality was poor (many scraped or irrelevant pages, Reddit tool timed out, a few sites blocked), so thin spots are flagged. Anything marked [inference] is my own reading or background knowledge that I could not confirm from a fetched source this session. Quotes are limited to short phrases.

## 1. Papers, Please (Lucas Pope)

**What it is.** 2013, PC (later mobile/Vita). Border-checkpoint inspector in a fictional Eastern Bloc state, 1982. Roughly 31 in-game days of story mode, a few hours, plus endless modes. 20 endings [wiki].

**The mundane layer.** Compare each traveler's papers to that day's rule sheet, stamp or deny, order interrogations, fingerprints, body scans. At day's end, pay minus citations, then budget rent, food and heat for your family [wiki].

**The wrong layer.**
- Each day's rule set gets heavier; relations with neighboring countries sour and new bans and required documents appear [wiki]. The same desk gets more cluttered and slower.
- Mistakes arrive as faxed citations after the traveler has gone, so harm is abstract and delayed [wiki].
- Moral pressure sits inside the routine: a plea costs you seconds, and seconds are food. Critics frame it as making attention to each person expensive, so names blur into the queue [critic].
- The family status screen is deliberately plain, so the game does not tell you how to feel [dev].
- Recurring named entrants break the stream of strangers (a repeat visitor, a person asking after a missing spouse) and then get a verdict day [wiki, partial].
- Body scanner added late made the inspector's intrusion explicit [dev].

**Most-cited moment.** The recurring entrants whose papers are almost right, and the faxed consequence. The repeat visitor everyone calls the favorite is the usual pick [player report, weak: wiki snippets only].

**Borrowable for Noted.**
1. Faxed/printed consequence after the fact: a "Notice of Variance" slip arrives under the door next day for something you did not notice you did. Cheap (a texture and a text line).
2. A rule sheet that changes quietly between days; one rule contradicts yesterday's with no comment.
3. One recurring "named" item in the queue (a mail item addressed to someone, same handwriting each day) with a slightly different state each appearance. No person on screen needed.
4. Keep the money screen plain and neutral; let numbers do the guilt.

**Avoid.** Branching dilemmas and a large cast; the game's weight comes from faces, which Noted cannot show.

## 2. Lethal Company (Zeekerss)

**What it is.** Early access Oct 2023, PC. Up to four co-op players as contract employees salvaging scrap from abandoned moons. Open-ended; runs end on quota failure or wipe [wiki].

**The mundane layer.** Loot scrap, return to the ship before the autopilot leaves at midnight, sell to the Company, meet a rising quota every three days, buy gear and decorations at the terminal [wiki].

**The wrong layer.**
- Corporate framing stays deadpan while the work is lethal: the quota is the only stated goal, and failing it ejects you into space [wiki].
- Rules-based monsters turn the player's routine into a hazard: the Coil-head freezes only while watched, so every glance away is a cost; the Jester winds up and hunts the whole facility; the Mimic hides as ordinary furniture and boxes, so touching an object becomes risky [player report, low-quality guides; wiki for Jester].
- Proximity voice chat is the main sound-based dread engine [wiki].
- Comedy is deliberately mixed in, which makes the horror hit sharper [critic].
- Escalation is by moon choice (more dangerous moons pay more), not by script [wiki].
- Furniture secretly affects a luck value [wiki].

**Most-cited moment.** Being separated from the group and hearing the Jester's music box start, or discovering a "chair" was a Mimic [player report, weak].

**Borrowable for Noted.**
1. Make an ordinary prop untrustworthy: one coffee station or mailbox slot that is subtly wrong (the label is mirrored, the date is a day ahead). Costs one material swap.
2. Corporate deadpan on the ship-terminal equivalent: the night shop uses cheerful copy for sinister items.
3. A hidden stat bought cosmetically (a desk plant changes what the Complaint Terminal presets say). Reward curiosity quietly.
4. Audio cue as the only warning (winding sound when a job is about to go wrong).

**Avoid.** Chasing monsters, co-op voice chat, and any hostile entity that "appears". Noted forbids a visible other.

## 3. Mouthwashing (Wrong Organ)

**What it is.** Sept 2024, PC (console ports listed for late 2026). First-person psychological horror on a wrecked freighter; length not stated on the store page; most reports say a few hours [store, inference].

**The mundane layer.** Work-adjacent exploration and item puzzles: you walk the ship, talk with the crew, and do small tasks while the company's layoffs hang over everyone. Some food-related minigames sit near body horror [critic]. Pre-crash scenes follow the crew's routine on a delivery run for a courier company [wiki]. The page does not confirm specific chores.

**The wrong layer.**
- Chronology is scrambled: scenes jump between weeks and months before and after the crash, with transitions that imitate a console crash so you briefly think the game broke [wiki].
- On-screen objectives are used to build suspense and subvert game convention [critic].
- The corporate frame (final delivery, layoffs, a mascot-branded shipment) carries satire and black comedy [critic].
- Helplessness: the captain is sedated and cannot speak or move; the player is made complicit by feeding or being fed [wiki].
- Late hallucinations (ship as maze or graveyard, a mock birthday party with the dead crew, the mascot confronting you) escalate from the corporate setup [wiki].
- Low-poly look makes abstraction do the work [wiki].

**Most-cited moment.** The mock birthday party with the dead crew and the forced meal; critics also single out the body horror [wiki, critic].

**Borrowable for Noted.**
1. Fake-crash transition between days: a half-second hard cut to black with a system-style error line, then a slightly changed room.
2. An on-screen objective that quietly lies (the task list says "Restock coffee: 3/3" and you only did two).
3. A company mascot graphic that appears in progressively wrong places (no human, so allowed).
4. Non-linear memory fragments via the evidence items: a drive label dated after the day you are on.

**Avoid.** Rape/assault themes, abuse narratives, gore; the heavy interpersonal cast is the engine and does not fit.

## 4. Home Safety Hotline (Night Signal Entertainment)

**What it is.** Jan 16, 2024, PC (later consoles). 1996-set call-center sim on a Windows 95 style desktop. Short, about one in-game week plus the Sept 2024 Seasonal Worker DLC [wiki, critic].

**The mundane layer.** Clock in, take a call, listen, search the hazard database, pick the match; end-of-shift score; disciplinary call if low [wiki].

**The wrong layer.**
- Early calls are bugs and prank calls; later calls shift to supernatural hazards in the same flat helpline register (a caller who no longer recognises his dead wife's face, an unseen cleaner in the house) [wiki, critic].
- Distorted phone audio, scratching, howling, singing in the background; quiet before each call [critic].
- A wrong answer triggers a follow-up call that shows the consequence [wiki].
- Network errors make the log unreadable and force answers from memory [critic].
- Emails from former employees urge you to leave; video clips on the desktop hint something is wrong [critic].
- Interface and sound distortion grows over the run [critic: Polygon, via wiki].
- Failing enough turns the employee into a mouse and ends the game [wiki].
- A late invitation to a trial ("descension") reframes the employer [wiki].

**Most-cited moment.** The slow-building weirdness of individual callers; the transformation into a mouse is the standout payoff [critic, wiki]. No single scene dominates.

**Borrowable for Noted.**
1. Emails or notes from "former trainees" on the terminal telling you to leave, written by name-only authors.
2. Mid-run reference binder failure: the thermostat-log or rota page becomes smudged/unreadable one day and you have to rely on memory.
3. Quiet before each job starts (a deliberate 2-second room-tone pause).
4. Consequence call after wrong answers: a short audio-only follow-up (one voice, no body), only if voice is allowed.

**Avoid.** A reveal that the employer is a specific supernatural species; it turns dread into lore delivery.

## Patterns across these four

- Consequences arrive late and indirectly (faxed citations, follow-up calls). Delay makes ordinary acts feel suspicious in retrospect.
- The most effective wrongness lives in the same UI as the mundane: rule sheets, logs, objectives, databases. When the tool you trust lies, dread follows.
- Escalation is staged: ordinary first, off second, hostile third. Every game keeps a stretch of purely normal work before the first anomaly.
- Corporate language stays cheerful while the content darkens (deadpan quotas, mascots, helpline register).
- Absence and prop-level untrustworthiness (a mimic chair, a plain status screen, a silent line) cost almost nothing to build.
- A repeated named presence, seen only through traces, creates attachment more cheaply than a full character.
