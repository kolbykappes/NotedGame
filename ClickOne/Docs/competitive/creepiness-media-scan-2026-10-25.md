# Creepiness from other media: The Magnus Archives, Severance, Good Omens

*2026-10-25. Companion to [creepiness-toolkit-2026-10-25.md](creepiness-toolkit-2026-10-25.md). Method: web search plus five article fetches (Reactor essay on Magnus, Salon on Lumon's language, Metropolis and Slashfilm on Severance's design); the Hollywood Reporter fetch redirected and was not read. Sources are thin for the Magnus sound design and for Good Omens, so each claim carries a tag: [src] from a fetched or searched source, [bg] from general background knowledge not checked here, [inf] my inference. Everything is paraphrased. Nothing here is a design decision.*

**Reading note:** our own premise is already close to Severance (Dharma-style company, mundane work, a cheerful corporate voice). Borrow the techniques, not the signature pieces (named founder, four-part doctrine, waffles, finger traps, the dance reward). ## The Magnus Archives (horror anthology podcast, 2016-2021)

**What it is.** An archivist works through a backlog of "statements" at a paranormal research institute. Each episode is one testimony, dictated, with a tape-recorder framing [src].

**The mundane layer.** Procedure. Assistants verify claims through interviews, site visits and financial records, so the uncanny arrives through ordinary admin and feels grounded [src].

**Techniques**
- **Secondhand testimony.** The horror reaches you filtered through a witness, a recording, a form. You never see the event [src].
- **Recorder framing.** Each episode opens with a tape-recorder click before the statement and the whole thing is built to feel like a recovered archive tape [src].
- **Unanswered questions early.** Early statements offer almost no explanation; each answer raises new questions, so the unknown stays bigger than the known [src].
- **A three-stage escalation** [src]: (1) *campfire*: distant, happens to other people, shared dread; (2) *isolation*: the danger moves inside the institute, paranoia and mistrust; (3) *existential*: answers become fuel for complicity and loss, with quiet kindness placed against the bleakness so it hits harder.
- **A taxonomy of fears** (watching, emptiness, repetition and so on) that gives every statement a family; the horror is categorised like records [src, partial; the sources disagree on count and names].
- **Clean-ish opening.** Statements begin with ordinary context (a job, a flat, a shop) before the intrusion [src, from a commentary piece; partly my synthesis].

**Most-cited moment.** Not one scare; the sources point to the quiet, personal statements late in the run (the Reactor essay singles out "Far Away") as the hardest-hitting [src].

**Borrowable for Noted**
1. **Door-log "statements".** Short flat entries, each ending with a verification line: "Corroborated: partial. Location visited: none on record." Cheap text, strong feel. [inf]
2. **A recorder-style click** before every log or card, so the log feels like an official record someone kept. One sound asset. [inf, from the framing technique]
3. **Three-phase oddity curve** matching the Magnus phases: (a) evidence that things happened to *someone else*; (b) things addressed to *you* (your badge number, your handwriting on a log you do not remember writing); (c) you were the previous user. Maps onto the rank ladder. [inf]
4. **Fear families as the oddity families.** Our toolkit says 20-30 oddities in 8-10 families; give the families fictional names and a fixed theme each (being recorded, being absent, repeating, being counted, and so on) so oddities rhyme without explaining. [inf]
5. **Verification as the horror.** Let the player's own filing jobs be the process that confirms the wrong thing ("Reading logged. Reading corroborated by nobody."). [inf]

**Avoid.** Long spoken exposition (it is an audio medium; we have toasts and cards), and explaining the Entities. Their strongest early quality is that nothing is explained [src].

## Severance (TV, 2022-)

**What it is.** Office workers whose work and home memories are surgically separated do puzzle-like data work for a company that treats the job as devotion [src].

**The mundane layer.** Sitting at a desk grouping numbers on a screen, with perks for hitting targets [src].

**Techniques**
- **Restraint.** Stay close to recognisable reality, with just enough strangeness to unsettle. The designer began from a plain sitcom-style office and pushed it from there [src].
- **Space.** Plain white walls and a restrained palette; long repeating white corridors; very large rooms with *low ceilings*; a single structural column in the middle of a work area; custom desks with only the essentials, such as one phone and one pen; midcentury modernist forms from corporate campuses [src]. No windows and stark fluorescent light [src, secondary].
- **Devices that do not make sense.** Late-1990s-style CRT monitors that are touchscreens, a huge trackball beside a chunky bright keyboard; the designer wanted a device that does not make a lot of sense, to reinforce a purgatory feel. The on-screen images were real and functional [src].
- **Work you cannot interpret.** The team sorts numbers by how they feel, never told what the numbers mean; like the audience, they do not know what they are filing [src].
- **Language as control** [src, Salon]: invented jargon and acronyms for ordinary events; project names that sound important and are never explained; a founder's doctrine that gives corporate principles the feel of a faith; invented folklore used to deflect a serious violation; euphemism (a fired worker is on a long cruise, rebellion is a minor disturbance); elaborate, indirect phrasing that buries plain meaning; a self-help book revised so workers accept control; policing of speech itself. Shared jargon divides insiders from outsiders, and severed workers have only company texts as their reality.
- **Rewards as control.** Hitting a quota earns a forced-fun reward ritual; the sources describe the rewards as hollow and infantilising [src; one fan-made prop shows the reward framed as an award for reaching 75% of a file].

**Most-cited moments.** The sources point more to the cheerful reward rituals and the corridor-and-column office than to any single scare. I did not find a sourced "most unsettling moment" [gap].

**Borrowable for Noted**
1. **Low ceilings in the big room, one column in the middle.** Our server hall or office could get this for almost no art. Long corridor repetition is already in the layout. [inf, from the design notes]
2. **Desks with one phone and one pen.** Remove props instead of adding them; a bare workstation reads as watched. [inf]
3. **A device that does not make sense.** A patch panel with a seventh port that is blank and labelled; a thermostat with a dial that goes past its scale. Cheap geometry. [inf]
4. **A sort mini-game whose criteria are never told.** The player learns by feel which pile is right. Our mail game uses FILE / SHRED / OUTBOX; an unlabelled-criteria variant is the cheap step toward Severance's numbers game. [inf]
5. **Original doctrine and euphemism layer.** Wall maxims and card text in an invented house voice; euphemism for the pull and for absence ("reassigned to the quiet wing"). Write it as our own org's voice, flat and indirect. [inf]
6. **Recognition card after a promotion.** A pleasant forced-fun moment (a small, odd, sincere perk) that the player did not ask for. Reward as a control and as a tell. [inf]
7. **Jargon never defined.** Task names and code words that recur without explanation, so the player learns the world by exposure. [src + inf]

**Avoid.** Copying Lumon's signature pieces (see the reading note). Also avoid the two-selves premise; it carries the show and does not fit a game where nobody appears.

## Good Omens (novel, 1990; TV, 2019-)

**What it is.** A comic novel about an angel and a demon who like the world too much to see it end, set largely in an idyllic English village [src, Wikipedia; the rest of the section is mostly [bg] and [inf] because searches returned little craft analysis].

**The mundane layer.** English village life, a bookshop, ordinary children [src].

**Techniques**
- **Cosmic stakes in a small ordinary place.** Lower Tadfield is the setting for the end of the world [src].
- **Institutions as bureaucracies.** Hell is run as an organisation; one demon is titled "Lord of the Files" [src]. Heaven and Hell are described as parallel and alike [src, interpretive reviews]. Orders arrive and are signed for [src].
- **The Arrangement.** An angel and a demon quietly offset each other's work to avoid effort [src].
- **Prophecies that are accurate and almost useless**, readable only by their intended person, time and place [src].
- **Comic understatement.** Crowley's fall is described as a casual drift downward, not a plunge [src].
- **Footnote voice** [bg]: a dry narrator who undercuts the drama in asides. The Wikipedia page only mentions footnotes in connection with translations, so this is not confirmed here.
- **Ordinary people decide the outcome.** The children, not the immortals [src].

**Most-cited moment.** The sources are about tone, not scares; none supplies one [gap]. Good Omens is not a horror source, so its value is the *comic* half of the mundane-versus-wrong contrast.

**Borrowable for Noted**
1. **The Arrangement as a trace of someone.** A task already half-done by someone who is not here: the coffee already restocked, a checkmark already made, a reading already logged in a hand that is not yours. Evidence of a counterpart who offsets your work without appearing. [inf]
2. **Prophecy-style documents addressed to you.** A note on the cot: "For the next associate. Do not read before the third week." Accurate, cryptic, useful only later. [inf]
3. **Footnote voice on forms.** Asterisk lines in small print under cards ("*Pending review.") that are slightly too frank. Cheap text. [bg + inf]
4. **Puns on titles.** Dry job titles in the Lord-of-the-Files register ("Custodian of Records", "Officer of Loose Ends") keep the mundane half funny so the wrong half lands. [inf]
5. **Understatement for the big oddities.** The strangest thing gets the shortest, flattest line. [inf]

**Avoid.** Letting the comedy win. Humour is the mundane register that makes the dissonance work; a gag rate that is too high defuses dread. Keep one joke-shaped register (the corporate voice) and leave everything else deadpan. [inf]

## What these three add that the 20 games did not

| New idea | From | Why it is new |
|---|---|---|
| **Statements with a verification line** (secondhand testimony as the log format) | Magnus | The games gave logs; this gives them a *grammar* |
| **A sound that makes records feel recovered** | Magnus | One asset, high effect |
| **Campfire, then you, then you were the previous one** | Magnus | A phase curve we can pin to rank |
| **Jargon never defined; a house voice with doctrine and euphemism** | Severance | A writing system, not just a poster |
| **Reward ritual as control** | Severance | Fits the tool locker and promotion cards |
| **Remove props instead of adding; low ceilings, one column; devices that do not make sense** | Severance | Geometry-only scares |
| **Sort by unstated criteria** | Severance | A mini-game variant that needs no new art |
| **A counterpart's work already done** | Good Omens (inferred) | Evidence of someone with no one on screen |
| **Prophecy notes; footnote voice** | Good Omens | Cheap text, adds the comic register |

**Caveat on the order of confidence:** Magnus structure and Severance design are sourced; the Severance mini-game parallel, all Good Omens tone points beyond Wikipedia, and every "Borrowable" line are my inference.
