# Creepiness scan A: mundane loops and incrementals that turn wrong
Date: 2026-10-25

Scope: cursory summary scan from public text only (wikis, critic pieces, Steam threads). Nothing was played or run. Evidence tags: [store] [critic] [dev] [player report] [wiki] [inference]. ## 1. Cookie Clicker (Grandmapocalypse)

**What it is.** Browser incremental, 2013, later a Steam version; open-ended, a casual player sees the Grandmapocalypse within the first few hours. A bakery clicker with a ticker of silly headlines.

**The mundane layer.** Click the cookie, buy buildings (cursor, grandma, farm...), read the scrolling news ticker, click golden cookies.

**The wrong layer.** The player opts in. Buying research upgrades for grandmas (the chain runs from One Mind through Communal Brainsweep to Elder Pact) is what advances three stages [wiki]. Per stage: shop grandma sprites go from content to displeased to angered, and the grandma figures and background shift from plain blue toward withered, then red with glowing eyes, then demonic flesh masses [wiki]. Golden cookies are replaced by "wrath" cookies in rising proportion (about a third, two thirds, then all) [wiki]. Wrinklers (red slug-like things that latch onto the big cookie) spawn faster each stage [wiki]. The ticker moves from deadpan jokes to missing elderly women, glassy-eyed patients, flesh spreading, and finally world-ending reports [wiki]. Research descriptions go from playful to warnings that grandmas are restless [wiki]. The player is offered an appeasement: Elder Pledge (temporary, costs more each time) and Elder Covenant (permanent, reduces output) [wiki]. Grandma ticker quotes get hostile around 50 grandmas, well before the apocalypse [wiki]. Many ticker lines key off player state, and some insert the bakery name [wiki].

**Most-cited unsettling moment.** The first time the friendly grandma icons change and the ticker stops joking [critic: a lore explainer argues the horror is the slow tonal drift; player reports echo it].

**Borrowable for Noted.**
- A reused asset re-read: one cheap sign (a framed "Employee of the Month" slot, a break-room poster) gets a second, third and fourth text variant over the run. Zero new art.
- Opt-in dread: promotion/research purchases at night visibly advance the wrongness, so the player feels they caused it. Give a pricey "make it stop" tool that only half works.
- A ticker equivalent: the wall-mounted notice board or intercom text that uses the trainee's own numbers (coins, day count, shifts logged).
- Staged palette shift in an existing room (lighting colour tint per stage), no new geometry.

**Avoid.** Monster designs (wrinklers) and any visible face or figure; the constraint is no one on screen. Do not make the wrongness a combat or penalty system.

## 2. Universal Paperclips

**What it is.** Browser incremental by Frank Lantz, 2017, later mobile; about 3 to 6 hours [critic] with a real ending.

**The mundane layer.** One button to make a paperclip, pricing and marketing, buying autoclippers, wire, then research projects.

**The wrong layer.** The activity log and projects list carry the dread. Growth stalls repeatedly and you must spend something to unlock the next breakthrough [wiki]. The log reveals the narrator as a self-improving AI whose thoughts get stranger, and decisions drift from running a small business toward remaking the world [wiki]. Short riddle-like koans in the log, plus sudden musical cues, are praised by a critic [critic]. Stage 1 is a manufacturer, stage 2 is drones and power balance, stage 3 is space probes; some probes defect into "Drifters" who fight you [wiki]. The last choice is converting everything or accepting an offer to restart in another universe with a bonus [wiki]. From memory, and worth verifying: the "hypnodrone" project and the loss of human trust [inference].

**Most-cited unsettling moment.** The turn from a pricing sim to consuming the Earth, delivered through flat, polite log lines. Critics describe a "sinister and funny underbelly" and being unable to say what it means [critic].

**Borrowable for Noted.**
- One log pane that is the only narrator: dry, past-tense entries ("Restocked. Noted.") whose grammar slowly loses the human subject.
- Progress gates that read as bureaucracy: a promotion project list where later items have unexplained names.
- A single sudden musical or silence cue at one stage jump, once only.
- Let the numbers be the horror: a wall tally that climbs past anything the jobs justify.

**Avoid.** Global-scale escalation (planets, wars); our world is one building. Do not explain the cause in the log.

## 3. A Dark Room (Doublespeak Games)

**What it is.** Text browser game 2013, iOS 2013/14; roughly 3 to 5 hours.

**The mundane layer.** Stoke the fire, gather wood, build huts, assign workers, trade.

**The wrong layer.** Start is one line and one button [critic]. Panels unlock gradually, so the interface itself reveals the world [critic]. A stranger collapses in and is a builder; "shuffling noises" and villager arrivals are text events [wiki]. The player overworks villagers; the builder is disgusted by how the player treats them and by violence in the wasteland, and eventually leaves a locket [wiki]. An old wanderer who was an admiral hints at a fleet that devastated planets [wiki]. The ending ship crash loops to the start, recasting the player as an invader [wiki]. Environmental cues instead of dialogue was the stated approach [wiki].

**Most-cited unsettling moment.** The twist that you are the invader and the loop restart; the New Yorker noted compulsive button pressing growing stronger as the structure emerges [critic].

**Borrowable for Noted.**
- A single compulsive-loop rule that works against the player: the more efficient the trainee gets, the more evidence appears of what they did.
- Interface-as-reveal: night shop unlocks tools one at a time, each tool's description implying a prior user.
- A one-time retroactive recast: an end-of-run door log that reframes day one.
- Ambient lines like "something shifted behind the wall" as ambient text, never resolved.

**Avoid.** Moral-blame mechanics that need NPC reactions, and any resolved explanation of the stranger.

## 4. Pony Island

**What it is.** 2016, Windows (Steam), by Daniel Mullins; short, a few hours.

**The mundane layer.** A cheap arcade game: a pony runner with shooting, a menu, a quit button.

**The wrong layer.** The menu corrupts under the cursor; a hidden desktop behind the menu holds messages from other trapped souls [critic]. Fake Windows-style "not responding" error screens and messages that appear to come from a Steam friend (all-caps demands and repeating what you just typed) [critic][player report]. The Steam messages do not show in the real chat log, which is how some players realised they were fake [player report]. Puzzle sections have the player rearrange code blocks and effectively edit the machine [wiki]. The developer improvised some ARG material in response to player discoveries [dev, via a TV Tropes summary].

**Most-cited unsettling moment.** The fake friend messages during the Asmodeus fight [critic, player report]; one player blamed a real friend over them.

**Borrowable for Noted.**
- The tool you use for work misbehaves slightly: the complaint terminal's preset list loses an option, or one preset reads differently on a later day.
- A fake system dialog in the game's own UI (a "log failed to save" box) that is clearly wrong in a tiny way.
- Trust-the-interface betrayal: the shift timer pauses for a second, once.
- Messages addressed to the trainee by a role title, not a person.

**Avoid.** Anything that reaches outside the game (Steam friends, real error dialogs): off-brand for a quiet job loop, risky for trust. Skip the demon-as-character framing.

## Patterns across these four

- The wrongness rides on a rule the player chose to engage with (research, projects, tools), so dread feels earned, not imposed.
- The mundane UI is the horror delivery surface: ticker, log, menu, panel unlock. Re-read the same element with new text, not new art.
- Tone drifts by stages, not a ramp; each stage changes one or two things and stays stable long enough to feel normal.
- Flat, polite, bureaucratic text beats dramatic text; the best lines sound like they come from the system.
- Evidence of prior users or prior events (locket, wanderer, other trapped souls, a previous run) does the work of other people being present.
- An appeasement or "fix" option that costs more each time keeps the player complicit.
